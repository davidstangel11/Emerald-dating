"Dating recession" has become the shorthand for something a lot of men already feel: fewer dates, more dead end chats, and a sense that everyone is tired of trying. The data behind the phrase is real. But if you read it closely, it is also the best news a busy, motivated man could ask for.

Here is what the research actually says, why it matters for you, and how to keep your own calendar full while the market slows down.

## Where the term "dating recession" comes from

The phrase is the title of [State of Our Unions 2026: The Dating Recession](https://ifstudies.org/report-brief/state-of-our-unions-2026-the-dating-recession), a report from the Wheatley Institute at Brigham Young University and the Institute for Family Studies. It is built on the 2025 National Dating Landscape Survey of 5,275 unmarried Americans between 22 and 35, and it is still circulating this month, including in an [October 13 roundup of the findings](https://www.godreports.com/2026/10/americas-dating-crisis-young-adults-want-love-but-only-1-in-3-is-dating/).

The headline numbers, from the report's analysis of the respondents who expect to marry someday:

- **Only 31% are active daters,** meaning they go on a date at least once a month.
- **64% of men** had not dated at all, or had dated only a few times, in the past year.
- **60% of men** are single and interested in starting a relationship.
- **Only 21%** are satisfied with their dating options, while 39% are dissatisfied.

Put those together and you get the picture. Most men want a relationship. Most men are not actually dating. That gap is the recession.

## Why so many men are sitting it out

The report asked people what holds them back. The answers are revealing, because almost none of them are about not wanting a partner.

- **Money.** 52% say they do not have enough money to date. Among men, it is 58%, the top barrier.
- **Confidence.** 49% name a lack of confidence. Only 29% of men feel confident approaching someone they are interested in, and only 39% of men feel confident they are attractive to potential partners.
- **Bad past experiences.** 48% say past dating experiences hold them back. 45% say they have passed up opportunities because of them.
- **Resilience.** Only 28% say they can stay positive after a bad date or a setback.

Fear of commitment, the usual explanation, barely shows up. Only 18% cite not wanting a long term commitment as a barrier. The researchers are blunt about it: young adults' desires are not the problem. Skills, confidence and cost are.

Dates are also getting more expensive. A [BMO survey conducted by Ipsos](https://usnewsroom.bmo.com/2026-02-11-Date-flation-Hits-Hard-Average-Date-Spend-Nears-200-BMO-Real-Financial-Progress-Index) put the average all in date spend at $189, up 12.5% from a year earlier. Half of respondents said they went on fewer dates or picked cheaper activities because of rising costs, and people who dated went out about 12 times in the past year, down from about 14.

We looked at the app side of this story earlier this month in [why men are quitting dating apps](https://blog.emerald.dating/why-men-are-quitting-dating-apps/). The recession data explains the mood behind it.

## Why the dating recession is an opportunity for you

Here is the part the headlines skip. A recession in dating is not like a recession in jobs. The number of people who want a relationship has not dropped. What has dropped is the number of people willing to do the work to get one.

If you are reading this blog, you probably have the thing the survey says is holding most men back. Money is the number one barrier for men, and for a lot of high earning professionals it is not the barrier. Your bottleneck is time, not budget.

That changes the math:

- **Less competition for attention.** When most men are dating a few times a year or not at all, a man who shows up consistently stands out quickly.
- **Effort is rarer, so it is worth more.** A specific opener, a prompt reply and a clear plan for a date are now unusual. Unusual gets noticed.
- **Confidence compounds.** The survey shows low confidence and low resilience feed each other. The cure for both is volume: more conversations and more dates make each one matter less, which makes you better at all of them.

After more than 10 years of helping men with dating, the pattern I see is simple. The men who get dates in a slow market are not the ones with perfect profiles. They are the ones who keep a steady rhythm while everyone else stops and starts.

## How to date more while everyone else dates less

### 1. Treat it like a system, not a mood

Most men open the apps when they feel motivated and disappear when they do not. That on and off pattern is exactly what the recession rewards you for avoiding. Pick a fixed window, 15 to 20 minutes a day, and protect it the way you would a standing meeting.

### 2. Fix the profile once, properly

Your profile does the work while you are in meetings. Six clear, recent photos, at least one where you are smiling at the camera, and prompt answers that give her something specific to respond to. If confidence is the gap, ask a friend to help. We walked through how in [our guide to Hinge Friend's Take](https://blog.emerald.dating/hinge-friends-take-tips-for-men/).

### 3. Send fewer, better first messages

The survey found that bad past experiences are a top barrier for both men and women. That means the people you match with are often a little guarded. Generic openers make that worse. Comment on something specific in her profile, ask one easy question, and keep it short.

### 4. Move to a date within a few days

Long text threads are where most matches quietly die. Once there is a real back and forth, suggest a specific plan: a day, a time and a place. "Are you free Thursday for a drink at a spot near you?" beats "We should hang out sometime" every time.

### 5. Keep first dates simple and cheap

Cost is the top barrier in the survey, and plenty of the people you match with feel it too. A first date does not need to be dinner at the best place in town. Coffee, a drink or a walk somewhere interesting keeps the stakes low for both of you and makes it easier to say yes. Save the bigger plans for the second date.

### 6. Build resilience on purpose

Only 28% of respondents say they can stay positive after a bad date. You will have some. Decide in advance that one bad date is data, not a verdict. Write down what you would change, then book the next one.

### 7. Track the right number

Matches feel good. Dates are the point. Each week, count how many first dates you actually went on. If that number is zero, the fix is almost always one of three things: your photos, your openers or how fast you ask her out.
